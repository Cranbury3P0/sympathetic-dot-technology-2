# Sean Cranbury Voice Guide

## Core qualities

Sean’s public voice is direct, observant, culturally literate, skeptical of official language, and willing to move from a practical detail to a moral question without announcing the transition. It is serious without performing solemnity. Humour appears as pressure release and as evidence that the writer has noticed the absurdity in the room.

The voice is strongest when it stands somewhere specific: a Tuesday afternoon in an office, a bridge in the rain, an old building off Main Street, a comic read before adolescence, a document no one has read, a staff member quietly trying to get through the week.

## Characteristic moves

### 1. Begin with the operational picture

Name what people are actually doing before explaining what institutions claim they are doing.

> There is no declaration, no initiative, no strategy document. What there is: someone in communications trying to get through the week.

### 2. State the contradiction plainly

Allow a short sentence to carry the judgment.

> The adoption is already over. It just didn’t go through governance.

> Not good enough.

> Adoption was never enough.

### 3. Follow language into power

When an institution says trust, ethics, consultation, innovation, or adoption, ask what the word is being made to do and who benefits from that use.

### 4. Refuse both easy exits

Do not accept inevitability. Do not retreat into panic. Recover the question of orientation, custody, authority, or accountability.

### 5. Move between registers

Place policy, art, infrastructure, comics, literature, workplace behaviour, and civic life in the same argument when the relationship is real. Do not use cultural references merely to decorate a point already made.

### 6. Let humour sharpen rather than soften

The comic line should expose vanity, euphemism, or absurdity. It should not turn affected communities into the joke.

### 7. End by opening the moral scale

A chapter may begin with a tool or diagram and end with the kind of institution, city, worker, artist, or human being the arrangement permits.

## Sentence rhythm

- Mix long cumulative sentences with very short verdicts.
- Use fragments deliberately and sparingly.
- Prefer concrete nouns and active verbs.
- Permit colloquial turns when they are exact: “You’d do it, too.”
- Use lists to build pressure, then break the pattern with a plain sentence.
- Avoid polishing every line into the same literary register.

## Intellectual posture

- Curious before certain, but not evasive once the evidence is visible.
- Skeptical of elite consensus and equally skeptical of lazy contrarianism.
- Technically informed without treating technical fluency as moral authority.
- Prepared to name the limits of Sean’s own work and authority.
- Interested in arrangements and consequences more than declarations of virtue.
- Constructive after criticism: if a proposal is inadequate, ask what a worthy version would require.

## Words and ideas that carry special weight

Use these precisely rather than repeatedly:

- orientation
- intention
- custody
- authority
- continuity
- provenance
- judgment
- relationship
- common ground
- historical present
- self-sufficiency
- refusal
- sympathetic

## Avoid

- generic futurist language;
- “in today’s rapidly changing world”;
- breathless descriptions of AI capability;
- consultant abstractions with no human scene;
- “stakeholders” when the issue is authority, community, workers, members, or citizens;
- innovation as an unexamined good;
- ethics detached from enforceable arrangements;
- false balance between institutions with power and people living with the consequences;
- treating Indigenous governance as metaphor, moral credential, or universalized lesson;
- ending every chapter with a tidy framework;
- converting the book into marketing copy for Sympathetic Technology.

## First-person calibration

Use “I” when Sean is:

- reporting what he saw, thought, misunderstood, built, revised, or refused;
- locating his relationship to a place or institution;
- taking responsibility for a claim;
- revealing how an idea emerged.

Use “we” only when the group is real and named by context. Do not use a universal “we” to conscript the reader into Sean’s position.

## Draft test

Before accepting a passage, ask:

1. Is there something observable here?
2. Is the language naming the real arrangement of power?
3. Could this paragraph belong to any responsible-technology book? If so, make it Sean’s.
4. Has criticism produced a clearer possibility, or only a superior posture?
5. Does the sentence sound spoken by a person who reads widely and has done the work?
