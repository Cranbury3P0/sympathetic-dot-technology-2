# Chapter 11 Architecture

## Losers Built This: Towards a Cooler Internet

**Part:** IV — Building Sympathetically  
**Target:** 3,300–4,000 words  
**Status:** Architecture active; unwritten  
**Author:** Sean Cranbury

## Chapter function

This chapter opens the book’s constructive final part.

The earlier parts establish orientation, expose the politics hidden inside adaptation, and recover relationship, authority, and continuity. This chapter turns those ideas toward the communications environment itself. It argues that the internet people experience now is not nature, maturity, or the unavoidable endpoint of networked life. It is something particular people built and continue to finance, regulate, normalize, and defend.

The chapter must earn its turn from “this was built badly” to “we are allowed to desire and construct something cooler.”

## Central provocation

The internet is not uncool because ordinary people ruined it by arriving. It is uncool because powerful people and institutions converted human contact, cultural expression, curiosity, and public life into an extractive enclosure—and then presented their business arrangements as technological inevitability.

## Meaning of “losers”

The word points upward.

Possible targets include:

- executives who mistook domination for invention;
- investors who converted every social possibility into an advertising or surveillance market;
- political leaders who surrendered public infrastructure to private platforms;
- founders whose emotional limitations became product architecture;
- institutions that confused scale with legitimacy;
- professional optimists who kept insisting degradation was innovation;
- the people who made the network less strange, less local, less generous, less human, and less fun while becoming extraordinarily wealthy.

The chapter should make this direction clear through the argument rather than pausing to apologize for the title.

## Governing tension

How do we recover the possibility of a cooler internet without becoming nostalgic for an earlier network that was never as open, equal, innocent, or universally available as memory suggests?

## McLuhan hinge: two meanings of cool

Marshall McLuhan gives the chapter title a productive double meaning.

In *Understanding Media* (1964), “cool” is not a synonym for admirable, fashionable, or humane. McLuhan uses it to describe media that are comparatively low in supplied definition and therefore require greater participation from the audience. “Hot” media supply more information and leave less for the receiver to complete. The distinction is relative, unstable across contexts, and not a moral ranking.

The chapter can place this technical meaning beside Sean’s cultural meaning of cool:

- a space with style, freedom, difference, mystery, competence, and creative possibility;
- a space that does not beg for universal approval;
- a space people participate in because they can make something of it, not because a platform has engineered compulsion.

This produces the chapter’s sharpest conceptual question:

> Does today’s internet require meaningful human participation, or does it merely extract user activity?

The contemporary platform internet demands enormous participation in the ordinary sense: posting, liking, reacting, correcting, tagging, moderating, training, and producing. But much of that activity occurs inside structures users cannot meaningfully alter. It may be “cool” through its demand for completion while remaining politically hot: highly determined, concentrated, and controlled.

Generative AI intensifies the contradiction. It can make media hotter in McLuhan’s sense by filling blanks, completing language, resolving ambiguity, and delivering highly specified outputs. At the same time it relies on the accumulated participation and cultural expression of people whose agency over the resulting system is minimal.

A cooler internet in the chapter’s full sense would require both participation and agency. People would not merely complete the medium; they would possess meaningful authority over the spaces, rules, relationships, and artifacts their participation creates.

## McLuhan’s prescience

Avoid the simplified claim that McLuhan “predicted the internet.” The stronger and more defensible argument is that he anticipated many of the social and perceptual effects later intensified by networked computing:

- electronic interdependence and the “global village”;
- simultaneity and “all-at-onceness” rather than the linear sequence associated with print;
- the collapse of distance without the disappearance of conflict;
- technology as an extension that reorganizes human senses, relationships, and institutions;
- computers as instruments of retrieval, communication, feedback, tailored information, and environmental programming;
- the reciprocal relationship in which people shape technologies and become reorganized in order to serve them.

McLuhan did not need to describe browsers, feeds, or smartphones accurately to give the chapter a method: examine what the medium does to scale, pace, pattern, perception, and human association rather than becoming distracted by the content passing through it.

His global village should not be represented as a harmonious worldwide community. McLuhan understood the village as capable of intensified disagreement, surveillance, intrusion, conflict, and forced proximity as well as connection.

## Proposed movement

### 1. The thing in front of us

Describe the present communications environment in concrete terms:

- repetition;
- compulsory platforms;
- algorithmic hostility;
- synthetic language and content;
- collapsing distinctions among communication, advertising, entertainment, work, and surveillance;
- enormous physical infrastructure hidden behind frictionless interfaces;
- the feeling that the network can produce only more of itself.

### 2. People built it this way

Reject technological destiny. Identify the incentives, decisions, personalities, and institutions that made this version of the internet. Connect interface choices to business models and business models to arrangements of power.

Introduce McLuhan’s insistence that the medium’s structure matters more than the individual messages moving through it. Ask what kinds of people and relationships the platform form produces regardless of whether a particular post is funny, humane, radical, or true.

### 3. What was once genuinely exciting

Sean locates himself in the history:

- early encounters with the internet;
- publishing and cultural communities;
- discovery, weirdness, amateurism, friendship, autonomy, and the feeling of making a place rather than occupying a product;
- what the internet allowed that previous media did not.

Complicate the memory. Ask who was absent, unsafe, excluded, or invisible in the version Sean remembers.

### 4. The enclosure

Trace several moments or patterns through which open possibility became platform dependency. This does not need to become a complete history of the internet. Use a few experienced or well-researched transitions that Sean can describe with authority.

### 5. What “cooler” means

Define cool as a relationship among freedom, style, generosity, difference, competence, mystery, and the refusal to beg for universal approval.

Bring the two meanings together. A genuinely cooler internet should leave space for people to complete, interpret, shape, and transform their environments while also granting them authority over the conditions of that participation.

A cooler internet may involve:

- many smaller places rather than one compulsory public square;
- communities able to govern themselves;
- the ability to leave with one’s work and relationships intact;
- public and cooperative infrastructure;
- provenance and human authorship;
- pseudonymity where it protects experimentation or safety;
- identity where accountability requires it;
- less measurement;
- less frictionless sharing;
- fewer recommendations;
- more intentional discovery;
- tools that can end;
- spaces not optimized for growth;
- culture that does not need to become content;
- room for technically unnecessary beauty and weirdness.

### 6. Towards

End in direction rather than solution. “Towards” keeps the proposition open, collective, and revisable. A cooler internet cannot be delivered as another total platform. It has to be built through many arrangements that restore choice, custody, community, and the capacity to leave.

## Relationship to the book’s three terms

### Orientation

Understand how this internet was built, whose interests it serves, what earlier possibilities remain recoverable, and where Sean stands within its history.

### Adaptation

Refuse the claim that people must adapt endlessly to degraded platforms. Require systems to adapt to human communities, cultural practices, and the right to depart.

### Expression

Recover expression from the content economy. Expression is not inventory for a feed. It is how people make culture, establish difference, find one another, and create worlds that did not previously exist.

## Research requirements

- a selective history of platform enclosure and changes in dominant business models;
- examples of cooperative, public, federated, local, or intentionally small online communities;
- portability and interoperability as cultural as well as technical conditions;
- relevant writing on early internet counterculture and its exclusions;
- precise sourcing for any claims about companies, founders, algorithms, labour, infrastructure, or harms;
- careful use and contextualization of Audre Lorde if the master’s-tools material remains.
- close reading of McLuhan’s own hot/cool definitions rather than relying on popular summaries;
- distinguish McLuhan’s account of participation from democratic agency, ownership, or governance;
- verify the publication and context of any McLuhan quotation against a reliable edition or archival recording;
- describe McLuhan as prescient about the effects and relationships of electronic media, not as a literal inventor or precise technical predictor of the World Wide Web.

## Risks

- nostalgia presented as history;
- treating personal taste as political analysis;
- blaming users for adapting to the available environment;
- vague anti-platform rhetoric with no account of incentives or architecture;
- proposing a universal replacement that recreates the problem;
- letting “cool” become an aesthetic pose rather than a description of freedom, difference, and desirable social life;
- relying on cultural references without making them carry intellectual weight.
- treating “cool media” as McLuhan’s endorsement rather than an analytic category;
- calling the whole internet either hot or cool without specifying the platform, form, use, and context;
- romanticizing participation when the participation is coerced, uncompensated, or structurally powerless;
- overstating McLuhan’s prediction of the internet and weakening a stronger argument about his anticipation of its effects.
