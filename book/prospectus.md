# Book Prospectus

## Working title

**Orientation Before Adaptation**  
*A Sympathetic Philosophy of Technology, Organizations, and Human Intention*

## The book in one sentence

Before people and institutions adapt to powerful technologies, they must understand where they are, how they arrived there, what they intend to preserve, who has authority, and what kind of humans they still intend to be when the transformation is complete.

## Central argument

Technological change is routinely presented as an external force to which human beings and organizations must adapt. That framing removes the most important questions before the conversation begins. It treats adoption as inevitable, speed as wisdom, convenience as value, and resistance as a literacy problem.

This book proposes orientation as the prior discipline. Orientation brings history into the room. It clarifies language and intention. It identifies where power sits, which relationships carry obligations, what knowledge has been lost, and what must remain under human judgment. Only then does adaptation become a choice rather than a surrender.

“Sympathetic” names the method. A sympathetic system does not merely perform a function. It responds to the history, values, language, limits, and relationships of the people who must live with it. It is designed to preserve agency and increase the capacity of its users rather than making them dependent on the builder. Technology is part of this work, but never its moral centre.

## What the book is

- a philosophy of technology grounded in organizational and cultural practice;
- an intellectual autobiography told through problems, places, institutions, and tools;
- a defence of history, language, judgment, continuity, and human authority;
- an argument against technological fatalism without becoming an argument against technology;
- a practical account of how ideas become architectures: Controlled Intelligence, Meridian, and Resonance.

## What the book is not

- a corporate history of Sympathetic Technology;
- a trend report about artificial intelligence;
- a generic responsible-innovation manual;
- a memoir organized around career milestones;
- an anti-technology polemic;
- a promise that better design can resolve conflicts of power.

## Reader

The primary reader is an intelligent non-specialist who feels the pressure of technological change inside an organization, institution, cultural practice, or creative life. They may lead a nonprofit, work in policy or health care, make art, manage knowledge, serve on a board, or simply feel that the language of “adoption” has left out the part where a person is allowed to decide.

The reader does not need technical fluency. They need to recognize the experience of being told that the future has already been decided by people who will not bear its costs.

## Reader promise

The book will give the reader a language for distinguishing transformation from submission. It will help them ask better questions before choosing tools, recover their own institutional or personal history, identify where judgment and authority belong, and imagine technological arrangements that leave people more capable rather than less.

## Narrative stance

First person. Sean is not a neutral guide standing outside the argument. He is a participant: a publisher, cultural worker, consultant, technologist, reader, citizen of Vancouver, and person who has lived through several successive promises that a new communications system would make human life more open, democratic, efficient, or free.

Personal history enters when it reveals how the philosophy was earned. The manuscript should not turn every claim into autobiography. It should show the encounters, failures, enthusiasms, contradictions, and places that made the claims necessary.

## Formal structure

Four parts and an epilogue, with brief personal interludes between parts. Chapters move between situated scenes, philosophical argument, institutional examples, and the architectures developed through Sympathetic Technology.

Target length: 48,000–52,000 words, excluding notes and acknowledgements. At approximately 275–310 words per finished page, this supports a 150–180-page book.

## Governing question

**What kind of humans do we intend to remain while building systems that simulate us?**

The question is not answered with a list of virtues. It is answered through arrangements: who controls the infrastructure, whose history remains available, who can refuse, what can be audited, whether language still means anything, and whether people emerge from the system with greater judgment and capacity of their own.

## Recurring distinctions

- orientation before adaptation;
- governance before automation;
- translation before transformation;
- custody before convenience;
- continuity before productivity;
- presence before performance;
- authority rather than consultation;
- trust earned rather than leveraged;
- language intended rather than generated;
- architecture rather than application;
- self-sufficiency rather than lock-in.
