# Complete Book Architecture

## Length model

- Main chapters and prologue/epilogue: approximately 45,800 words
- Four brief interludes: approximately 2,800 words
- Total target: approximately 48,600 words
- Expected finished extent: 150–180 pages, depending on trim, typography, and notes

## Prologue — The Question the Market Will Not Ask

**Target: 2,500 words**

Open on a technology announcement, strategy diagram, or workplace moment in which the future has been presented as already decided. Introduce the governing question: What kind of humans do we intend to remain while building systems that simulate us?

Establish that this is neither a rejection of technology nor a guide to adopting it. It is a book about recovering the human authority to decide what technology is for.

Sources: “The Pope Has Entered the Chat,” “Frame After Frame,” home-page approach.

Personal material needed: Sean’s first encounter with the inevitability rhetoric around AI and the moment it became clear that the real problem was orientation.

# Part I — Orientation

## Chapter 1 — Orientation Before Adaptation

**Target: 3,500 words**

Define orientation as a human and organizational practice. Show how speed produces confusion and paralysis when no one can name the present. Distinguish adaptation chosen from adaptation imposed.

Key movement: noisy environment → present conditions → possible futures → path chosen with intention.

Sources: home-page approach; Harvard capstone; Controlled Intelligence principle “translation before transformation.”

## Chapter 2 — Connect to the Historical Present

**Target: 3,500 words**

Argue that history is active infrastructure. Institutional knowledge is scattered not only across files but across former staff, abandoned systems, unrecorded judgments, and decisions separated from their reasons. Recovering it is not nostalgia; it is the restoration of continuity and agency.

Sources: Meridian; home-page historical present; Resonance; papal institutional-history essay.

Personal material needed: an archive, organization, or project where recovering an earlier decision changed what became possible.

## Chapter 3 — Use the Language of Intention

**Target: 3,200 words**

Language is not a product delegated to a machine. It is how people establish common ground, reveal disagreement, carry history, and state what they are prepared to do. AI can generate words but cannot determine an institution’s meaning or intention.

Sources: home-page language principle; Meridian’s knowledge/judgment/voice rings; “Frame After Frame”; Resonance.

Personal material needed: publishing and editorial experience; a moment when the right phrase exposed a false agreement or enabled action.

### Interlude I — The Smell of Rain

**Target: 700 words**

A situated West Coast meditation on knowing where one is. Petrichor, territory, weather, distance, and the difference between location as metadata and orientation as relationship.

# Part II — Power

## Chapter 4 — The Adoption Is Already Over

**Target: 3,600 words**

Enter the office. Staff are already using AI because the economic pressure is real and the tools help. Leadership’s delay has not paused adoption; it has privatized the decision and transferred risk downward.

Reject the framing of workers as reckless. Governance must begin by understanding the conditions under which rational people act.

Sources: “The AI Frontier Is Inside Your Office.”

## Chapter 5 — Trust Is Not a Lever

**Target: 3,500 words**

Read institutional language closely. When governments and companies call trust a prerequisite for adoption, trust becomes an instrument used to move people toward a predetermined outcome. Distinguish trust requested, trust manufactured, and trust earned.

Sources: “Frame After Frame”; Vancouver data-centre essay; Harvard capstone.

## Chapter 6 — Governance Is an Arrangement

**Target: 3,400 words**

Policies cannot govern tools whose actual architecture contradicts them. Governance becomes real through custody, boundaries, authority, auditability, the ability to stop, and consequences when commitments are broken.

Controlled Intelligence appears here as one worked example of philosophy becoming infrastructure.

Sources: Controlled Intelligence pages and essay; Harvard capstone.

## Chapter 7 — The Right to Refuse

**Target: 3,200 words**

Recover refusal as evidence of judgment rather than a failure of literacy. Explore how institutions relabel a clear no as resistance, fear, or a translation problem. A meaningful process must permit the answer that the technology should not be used.

Sources: “Frame After Frame”; Harvard capstone questions; Vancouver protest essay.

### Interlude II — Tuesday Afternoon

**Target: 700 words**

A staff member, a deadline, a personal AI subscription, and a policy in a shared drive. A small scene showing the distance between governance as written and governance as lived.

---

# Part III — Relationship

## Chapter 8 — What an Organization Knows

**Target: 3,500 words**

Documents do not equal knowledge. An organization exists in the relationship among authoritative records, experienced judgment, and the voice through which it explains and acts. When those elements separate, the same question receives three incompatible answers.

Sources: Meridian Framework; historical-present principle.

## Chapter 9 — Continuity, Not Automation

**Target: 3,300 words**

Most valuable work does not need to run itself. It needs to retain its shape across interruptions. Explore memory, revision, editorial reasoning, return, and the difference between a system that performs the work and one that helps a person re-enter it.

Sources: Resonance Engine and Perkins example.

Personal material needed: the editorial relationship that revealed continuity as the condition of excellence.

## Chapter 10 — Authority Is Not Consultation

**Target: 3,400 words**

Distinguish being heard from having power. Consultation added to a predetermined design does not produce legitimacy. Use the Harvard capstone’s necessary correction and Vancouver’s data-centre proposals to examine governance, Indigenous authority, public consent, and the limits of Sean’s own position.

Sources: Harvard capstone; Vancouver essays; Cultural Embassy.

Requires careful external review and fact-checking. Indigenous governance must not be reduced to a general management lesson.

### Interlude III — A Desk That Remembers

**Target: 700 words**

A writer returns after three weeks. The desk remembers the conversation but has not written the next sentence. A miniature of sympathetic technology: continuity without replacement.

# Part IV — Building Sympathetically

## Chapter 11 — Losers Built This: Towards a Cooler Internet

**Target: 3,300 words**

Begin with an accusation and turn it into a design problem. The internet people now inhabit was not inevitable; it was built by particular people, incentives, companies, governments, and cultural failures. “Losers” names the powerful people who made the network smaller, meaner, more extractive, and profoundly uncool—not the people trapped inside it.

Move through early internet possibility, platform enclosure, repetition, the communications environment, Venom, and the poisoned texture of systems that turn people against one another while extracting value from their contact. Refuse nostalgia: the earlier internet carried exclusions, fantasies, and concentrations of power of its own. Recover what was genuinely valuable without pretending there was a lost digital Eden.

The second movement asks what would make an internet cooler now: smaller systems, human-scale communities, legible governance, user custody, creative weirdness, portability, real identities where appropriate and pseudonymity where necessary, less extraction, more local authority, and tools that help people leave rather than making departure impossible.

Sources: “The Master’s Tools Will Never Dismantle the Master’s Datacenter.”

Additional source material needed: Sean’s history with the early internet, publishing and online cultural communities, platforms he loved or abandoned, and specific moments when the network stopped feeling open and started feeling occupied.

## Chapter 12 — The Cultural Embassy

**Target: 3,300 words**

Move from critique to civic imagination. If technological infrastructure is built from cultural labour, consumes public resources, and occupies territory, what obligations should take architectural and governing form? Explore cultural authority, independent trusts, public space, ecological ambition, and the difference between an embassy and a mural in a lobby.

Sources: “A New Cultural Embassy”; “Time for Vancouver to Come Clean About AI.”

## Chapter 13 — Sympathetic Technology

**Target: 3,400 words**

Define the philosophy positively. A sympathetic technology responds to the people, history, values, limits, and relationships around it. It preserves custody, makes provenance visible, supports judgment, allows refusal, maintains continuity, and aims at the self-sufficiency of the people it serves.

Use Controlled Intelligence, Meridian, and Resonance as three expressions of one philosophy:

- boundary and accountability;
- alignment and organizational voice;
- continuity and creative presence.

Personal material needed: origin of the company and its name; what Sean means by being a West Coast studio; the moment these systems became one body of thought.

### Interlude IV — Books in Original Form

**Target: 700 words**

Reading, friendship, art, and life outside the feed. Not a nostalgic escape from technology, but a recovery of experiences whose value is not improved by being optimized.

---

## Epilogue — The Humans We Intend to Remain

**Target: 2,200 words**

Return to the governing question without pretending it has one permanent answer. The work of orientation is continuous because conditions change, knowledge grows, relationships shift, and power finds new language.

End with a modest proposition: the future remains open wherever people retain the ability to remember, name, judge, refuse, build, revise, and care for one another without asking a platform for permission.

## Structural safeguards

- No chapter should function as a brochure for a Sympathetic Technology offering.
- Every abstract claim should eventually touch a scene, object, document, place, or decision.
- Each part must include an instance where Sean revises or complicates his own position.
- Indigenous authority must remain specific to peoples, territory, and governance—not a metaphor.
- Critique must lead to an arrangement, possibility, or sharper question rather than ending in posture.
- Repeated principles should deepen in meaning; they should not be restated as slogans.
